A lifelong-learning program for adults with intellectual disabilities should do more than repeat school material. Learners need opportunities to express what they have understood in their own way and use it again in places such as a clinic, café, workplace, or community center. Nalkku is a monthly paper-based learning program that connects literacy, analogy learning, self-expression, and transfer into everyday life. Each month, learners develop ideas, record them by hand, and build a structure in which learning can carry into life.

“Lifelong education for people with developmental disabilities” is a broad expression that may include people with intellectual disabilities, autistic people, and others. This article focuses on adults with intellectual disabilities who have foundational reading and writing skills. It may inform broader discussion, but it does not assume that everyone within the developmental-disability category learns in the same way.

## Summary {#summary}

- Nalkku is a monthly program connecting literacy, expression, and everyday transfer for adults with intellectual disabilities.
- Each month moves through three stages: learning, expression, and connection.
- Materials offer three levels around the same topic so learners can participate together without being separated by ability.
- AI assists with drafts and personalization; people remain responsible for educational judgment and final review.
- Progress records include forms of expression, needed prompts, and changes in everyday application, not only correct-answer rates.

## Lifelong learning should connect with life {#lifelong-learning}

The need to learn does not disappear after graduation. Adult life involves ongoing situations that require understanding and judgment: checking schedules, using money and time, interpreting health information, navigating relationships, and asking for help. Korea's Lifelong Education Act includes adult literacy, vocational development, liberal education, and civic participation within lifelong education and assigns public responsibility for expanding opportunities for people with disabilities.[2](#source-2)

Korea's National Center for Lifelong Education Promotion for People with Disabilities publishes surveys on programs, learners, and organizations.[1](#source-1) Within this broader field, Nalkku aims to provide one option through which adults with intellectual disabilities and foundational literacy skills can connect reading and writing with daily life.

## What Nalkku is {#nalkku-definition}

Nalkku is not a stack of worksheets designed simply to increase the number of reading questions completed. One monthly package brings together understanding, recording personal experience through speech, writing, or drawing, and carrying a learned relationship into another everyday situation.

Its central principle is analogy. Analogy is not about finding two things that look alike; it is about recognizing a similar relationship within different situations. Placing cases side by side and identifying a shared structure can support transfer of a learned principle to a new problem.[4](#source-4) This does not mean analogy activities alone improve everyday functioning for every learner. Familiar experience, language comprehension, and support requirements must be considered together.

## A month of learning, expression, and connection {#learning-cycle}

Each Nalkku month moves through three stages:

1. **Learning:** Read a short text, learn key vocabulary, and compare the relationships in two situations.
2. **Expression:** Move beyond selecting an answer and record personal ideas and experience through speech, writing, or drawing.
3. **Connection:** Ask, “What about my situation?” and “What could I try next?” and choose a small action to attempt in everyday life.

This cycle connects the success of understanding with the recognition that “I have a story to tell” and “I can use this somewhere else.” Transfer is designed explicitly into questions and activities rather than left to chance.

## Six tools with different roles {#learning-tools}

The components are not multiple copies of the same kind of worksheet.

- **Analogy workbook:** A tool for reading, identifying relationships, answering extension questions, and telling one's own story
- **Reading and writing sheets:** A reading tool supporting easy information, copying, and reading aloud
- **Routine challenge book:** A time-based tool for choosing a small goal and reflecting on practice and emotion
- **Free-expression notebook:** A tool for bringing out preferences and ideas without a predetermined correct answer
- **Nalkku News:** A social-connection tool for encountering another learner's story in accessible language
- **Facilitator record:** A documentation tool that carries needed prompts, forms of expression, and possible transfer into the next design decision

Sentences, choices, drawings, and practice records become a small paper portfolio. Its value is not primarily the quantity completed, but the accumulated experience of being a person who learns and leaves a record.

## Learning together at individual levels {#levels-and-udl}

Within one group, people differ in reading pace, sentence comprehension, speech, and writing. Nalkku keeps the topic and activity sequence shared while adjusting sentence length, vocabulary, number of options, relational complexity, and prompts across three levels.

The goal is not to separate people according to ability. It is to let everyone encounter the same topic through an accessible route. Universal Design for Learning recommends multiple pathways for engagement, access to information, and expression, along with graduated support and opportunities for transfer and generalization.[3](#source-3) Keeping pointing, choosing, speaking, writing, and drawing open as valid forms of response follows the same principle.

## AI behind the scenes, paper in front of the learner {#ai-and-paper}

The learner encounters paper. They can open it, read, write, color, attach something, and return to an earlier page. Paper allows a person to leave a trace at their own pace without screen transitions or automatic scoring.

Behind the scenes, AI assists designers in varying difficulty, developing interest-based examples, and organizing growth records. An AI draft is never distributed as-is. A person reviews adult-appropriate language, accuracy, difficulty, safety, and respect before deciding the final material. Learners' names, contact details, addresses, diagnostic information, counseling transcripts, and facial photographs are not entered into generative AI systems.

## Documenting growth beyond correct answers {#outcomes}

A correct-answer rate is one part of an activity result, not the whole result. Records can show whether a learner initially needed an example but later answered from options alone, expressed an idea through a drawing instead of speech, connected a text with personal experience, or recognized a similar principle in another place.

These records are not used to score the learner. They inform the next month's sentence length, prompts, and forms of expression. For an organization, they help explain the learning process. For the learner, they preserve choices and sentences that make growth visible over time.

## What Nalkku does not promise {#honest-boundaries}

Nalkku does not replace a formal curriculum, medical care, or rehabilitation. It does not promise dramatic change in a short period, and it does not claim that analogy activities increase intelligence.

What Nalkku seeks to create is a structure for repeated, meaningful participation. One person may write a paragraph; another may choose one sentence or leave one drawing. Beginning from the fact that pace and outcomes differ, the program provides continuing experiences of learning, expression, and connection and documents the context of change honestly.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

Lifelong learning for adults with intellectual disabilities is not remedial schooling after school age. It creates continuing opportunities to understand, express, and choose as an adult. Nalkku proposes a monthly structure that begins with accessible information and moves through analogy questions, self-expression, transfer into daily life, and a paper record. Its principle is simple on the surface, deep in design, and tangible in the learner's own hands.
